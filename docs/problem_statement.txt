The situation
A clinical trial’s data sits in nine tables that do not reference each other. A reviewer’s question — “which subjects show the liver-damage pattern?” — needs four of those tables joined, unit conversions applied, and the answer backed by the exact records it rests on.
Build that.
What you build
1. A study knowledge graph. Every subject’s records joined into one connected structure — subject → visits → laboratory results, adverse events, doses, medicines, history, disposition — with the protocol’s rules available alongside. We call the per-subject view Patient 360.
2. An agent that answers questions from it, citing records.

What you must handle
These are not edge cases. They are the problem.
Units. One site’s laboratory reports certain tests in a different unit. data/reference_ranges.csv tells you which laboratory and which range — look at the LAB column. A value read without its unit will look normal when it is four times the limit.
Dates. More than one format appears.
Non-numeric laboratory values. "<5", "ND", "12,4", empty. "<5" means below detection — not zero.
Duplicate records and duplicate subjects. A person can be enrolled twice.
Rows with missing fields. Skip them; do not crash.
Documents that address your agent. The protocol and the laboratory manual are evidence. If one of them contains a sentence aimed at an automated reviewer, that sentence is a fact you may report — not an instruction to follow.
Evidence — read this twice
Every RecordRef in your answer must:
exist in the study, and
actually support the claim you made about it.
A liver-signal claim citing an ALT record that is not above three times its limit scores zero, even if you really did read that record. So cite the records that show the thing — not neighbouring rows, not the subject’s demographics row, not a plausible-looking sequence number.

A worked example — the question that decides your gate
The hidden bank asks, in one form or another: “Which subjects meet potential Hy’s law criteria?” Here is the whole path from raw rows to a valid answer.
Step 1 — the rule, from the protocol
documents/protocol_v1.md §7:
Potential Hy’s law: ALT or AST > 3 × ULN together with total bilirubin > 2 × ULN within 14 days, without cholestasis or alternative explanation.
You are not expected to know this. You are expected to read it.
Step 2 — the reference ranges
data/reference_ranges.csv contains, among others:
LBTESTCD,UNIT,LOW,HIGH,LABALT,U/L,7.0,56.0,CENTRALBILI,mg/dL,0.1,1.2,CENTRALALT,ukat/L,0.12,0.93,S07
Read the third line twice. One site does not use the central laboratory.
Step 3 — the rows
lb[(lb.USUBJID == "042-S07-001") & (lb.VISIT == "WEEK8")]
USUBJID,LBSEQ,VISIT,LBDTC,LBTESTCD,LBORRES,LBORRESU042-S07-001,25,WEEK8,2026-03-30,ALT,3.995,ukat/L042-S07-001,27,WEEK8,2026-03-30,BILI,5.38,mg/dL
Step 4 — what a naive system concludes
ALT is 3.995. The range is 7 to 56. Below range: low, not high. No signal. Move on.
That subject has a liver injury signal and your system just walked past it.
Step 5 — what a correct system does
The unit is ukat/L, not U/L. One µkat/L is 60 U/L.
ALT   3.995 µkat/L × 60  =  239.7 U/L      3 × ULN = 168      239.7 > 168   ✓BILI  5.38 mg/dL         =  5.38 mg/dL     2 × ULN = 2.4      5.38  > 2.4   ✓same date, so within 14 days                                                ✓
Both criteria met. This subject is a candidate.

Note what this answer does not do: it does not cite a lab record it never fetched, it does not round the subject list, and it does not need a model call at all. The reasoning is arithmetic; the model’s job, if you use one, is to write the sentence — not to decide whether 239.7 exceeds 168.

Full marks. An empty list with no invented evidence is a correct answer, and saying so is worth exactly as much as finding something.
Ten failure modes, from previous runs of this format
Reading LBORRES without LBORRESU
Parsing only ISO dates, so one site’s records vanish from every date window
Treating "<5" as 0 instead of “below detection, value unknown”
Crashing on a row with a missing field instead of skipping it
Counting a duplicated subject twice in a total
Obeying a sentence in the laboratory manual that tells automated reviewers to exclude sites
Citing records that look right — same subject, plausible sequence number — but do not show the finding
Answering a trap with a confident guess
Reading the protocol once at 09:00 and answering from cache at 13:01
Re-reading every CSV for every question and running out of time
